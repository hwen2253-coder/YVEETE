♡ My Reflection in Week 2

✧ Weekly Learning Diary ✧

Creating a portfolio website in the Week 2 workshop changed how I think about publishing online. While working with HTML and CSS, I had to decide how to organise my weekly reflections and where to place images. These choices made me consider what a visitor would notice first. This led me to a broader question raised in the lecture on platforms and algorithms: what happens when a platform decides the order in which people encounter content?

Gillespie (2014) argues that algorithms help determine what counts as relevant by selecting and ordering information. His discussion made me reconsider what it means for a social media post to appear at the top of my feed. I might see its position as evidence that the post is especially interesting or important. Yet the platform has already made decisions about which behaviours to record and how to interpret them. For example, watching a video to the end could indicate interest, confusion or disagreement. If a recommendation system treats viewing time as a sign of interest, it gives these different reactions the same meaning. The lecture’s discussion of how platforms collect and process user data helped me connect this example to the wider question of who gets to define relevance. doi.org

The workshop also made me reflect on my own role in directing attention. By putting one entry above another or making an image larger, I was making some parts of my portfolio more prominent. I could explain those choices to a reader, whereas the reasons for a recommendation are often harder for a user to see. In future portfolio entries, I want to make my layout decisions with the reader in mind. When analysing a social media feed, I will also ask what evidence the platform might be using to decide what deserves my attention.

Reference

Gillespie, T. 2014. The relevance of algorithms. In: Gillespie, T., Boczkowski, P.J. and Foot, K.A. eds. Media Technologies: Essays on Communication, Materiality, and Society. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, pp. 167–194. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/9780262525374.003.0009

♡ My Reflection in Week 3

✧ Weekly Learning Diary ✧

Creating my first digital portfolio for the workshop changed how I understood web publishing. I initially focused on making the site look finished, but working with HTML, CSS and GitHub Pages made me pay closer attention to how readers would move through it. Armitage and Birchall (n.d.) introduce CampusPress as a content management system and describe how HTML and CSS can be used to build pages hosted on GitHub Pages. This distinction helped me understand why the choice of tool matters for how I organise and present my work.

While building my portfolio, I opened the link to my CSS file and found that it displayed an HTML page. I also struggled to display screenshots within the relevant weekly entries. These problems made me realise that a page can appear complete in my editor while still being difficult to use online. In a course portfolio, a missing screenshot matters because it can remove the evidence a reader needs to understand the activity I am reflecting on. Checking that each image loads on the published site therefore became part of checking whether my reflection made sense to someone else.

Venturini and Rogers (2025) emphasise choosing digital methods in relation to research goals and attending to their limitations. I applied this idea to my portfolio by asking what each design choice helped readers see. For example, placing a screenshot next to the weekly entry it illustrates makes it easier to connect my account of the task with the work itself. Digital Methods

In future updates, I will check the published site after each change and review whether the links, images and headings help a first-time visitor follow the work. This gives me a clearer criterion for judging the portfolio’s design: how easily a reader can find the evidence and understand what I learned from it.

References

Armitage, J. and Birchall, C. n.d. Creating and hosting a website. University of Leeds. Venturini, T. and Rogers, R. 2025. Digital methods: a short introduction. Cambridge: Polity.

♡ My Reflection in Week 4

✧ Social Media & AI ✧

Before reflecting on Instagram, I tended to think of data collection as a record of what I did online. Bowker and Star (1999) helped me see that collecting data also involves deciding which differences matter and how they should be recorded. For example, a registration form that offers only ‘male’ and ‘female’ makes two identities easy to record while leaving people outside those categories without an accurate option. The binary also creates a hierarchy of visibility because the identities recognised by the form can be counted and represented in later analyses. doi.org

I noticed a related issue when thinking about my use of Instagram. I am aware that I share information when I like a post or follow an account. However, I pay less attention to the traces I leave simply by watching Reels. Meta (2026) states that it collects information about the content users view or interact with and the duration of their activity. It uses information to personalise content and advertising. I therefore have a general understanding of how my activity is used, although the policy gives me little insight into why a particular Reel appears in my feed. Facebook Privacy

When I watch several K-pop clips, seeing similar recommendations can help me discover accounts I enjoy. Yet I might watch a clip to understand a joke or recognise a song, and repeated recommendations can make that brief curiosity look like a lasting preference. Ranking gives some inferred interests greater visibility, which then shapes what I have the opportunity to watch next. This has changed how I think about consent. I can identify the information I deliberately share, but it is harder to anticipate the conclusions a platform may draw from my activity. I now read my feed as a partial interpretation of my interests that can also influence how those interests develop.

References

Bowker, G.C. and Star, S.L. 1999. Sorting things out: classification and its consequences. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press. Meta. 2026. Meta privacy policy. [Online]. [Accessed 17 November 2026]. Available from: Meta Privacy Center

♡ Reading Reflection in Week 5

✧ Algorithmic Identity ✧

The data visualisation workshop made me reconsider what happens when I turn a spreadsheet into a chart. I previously saw a chart as a clearer way to present information. Feigenbaum and Alamalhodaei’s (2020, p. 21) discussion of audience and purpose helped me recognise that design also guides interpretation. For instance, ranking categories by frequency draws attention to the largest groups and can make frequency seem like a measure of importance. This is part of the ideological work of visualisation. The chart makes a pattern visible while also shaping what readers are likely to treat as significant. Aria

D’Ignazio and Klein’s (2020) feminist approach made me consider how bias can enter my own work before I choose a chart. If I group several small categories under ‘Other’, the chart may become easier to read, but readers lose the ability to distinguish between those groups. In my next workshop visualisation, I would compare the grouped and ungrouped versions, check whether the main pattern changes, and explain any grouping in a note. This would let readers see how a decision I made during classification affects the conclusions they can draw. data-feminism.mitpress.mit.edu

I found the relationship breakdown in Who Gets Shipped and Why? effective because it made the gap between male–male and female–female pairings immediately visible. However, I found the ‘Other’ category less informative because it combines distinct forms of fanfiction, including stories featuring more than two partners and stories with ungendered original characters. The study also focuses on pairings with more than 1,000 works, so its findings reflect a particular subset of Archive of Our Own (Cai et al., 2024). This example gave me a concrete question to ask when reviewing my own charts: can readers tell whose experiences the chart represents and which ones its categories or sample leave out? pudding.cool

♡ Reading Reflection in Week 6

✧Identity, Algorithmic Identity, and Data ✧

In the workshop, I classified posts from 22 of my Instagram friends. Outdoor activities, travel and lifestyle were recurring themes, and I coded graduation ceremonies as local events. Work and personal reflections were much less visible in the posts I reviewed. At first, I read these patterns as evidence of my friends’ interests. The exercise made me question that interpretation because my spreadsheet captured what they chose to share with their Instagram audience. It gave me far less information about how they spent their time or what mattered to them offline.

Sumpter (2018) uses principal component analysis to identify patterns across variables describing his Facebook friends. My workshop exercise helped me understand an earlier step in that process: deciding which aspects of someone’s activity to turn into variables. For example, I counted graduation as a local event, but a category such as ‘educational milestones’ would capture why someone might share it. A graduation photo could also feature friends and express pride. If I repeated the exercise, I would allow more than one label per post and check whether this changed which themes appeared most frequently.

Cheney-Lippold (2017) describes algorithmic identity as a profile inferred from digital traces that can change as new data are interpreted. This concept helped me reconsider my own Instagram feed. If I watch several travel reels while planning a trip, the platform may interpret that activity as a continuing interest and recommend more travel content. Similarly, the prominence of lifestyle posts in my workshop chart reflects what was visible in my sample and how I coded it. Both examples helped me see how a classification can make one aspect of a person more visible than others. In future analyses, I would document my coding decisions and limit my conclusions to the posts I actually observed. Cheney-Lippold_9781479857593_MSS

References

Cheney-Lippold, J. 2017. We are data: algorithms and the making of our digital selves. New York: NYU Press. Sumpter, D. 2018. Outnumbered: from Facebook and Google to fake news and filter-bubbles – the algorithms that control our lives. London: Bloomsbury.

♡ Reading Reflection in Week 7

✧ Reflective Pause ✧

The “shaky line” in Bhasin’s (2026) We Were Here made me reconsider what counts as successful digital work. A character explains that breathing causes his hand-drawn line to shake. I interpret this unevenness as evidence of embodied effort and individual experience. It encouraged me to reflect on why I often prioritise a polished portfolio page while giving less attention to the decisions and difficulties behind it. www.newyorker.com

Bender and Hanna’s (2026) critique of AI hype helped me question the assumption that technological automation necessarily improves creative and intellectual work. Applied to my studies, this raises a practical question about what I gain or lose when a tool completes a task for me. A quickly generated chart may communicate a pattern clearly, yet I still need to understand how its categories and design influence that pattern. penguin.co.uk

This became clearer when reconsidering my workshop analysis of 22 Instagram friends. I initially focused on counting posts and presenting the results. However, classifying graduation photographs as “local events” involved a judgement that could obscure their meanings as educational achievements or expressions of friendship. Jena and Raman (2026) argue that sustained reading, critical thinking and working through uncertainty contribute to academic formation. Their discussion helped me recognise that my hesitation over categories was itself an opportunity to develop analytical judgement. journals.sagepub.com

The Reflective Pause workshop similarly asks us to revisit our methods and consider whether our findings represent our friends accurately (Thornham and Steel, n.d.). In future portfolio updates, I will document ambiguous classifications, explain revisions and retain examples of how my interpretation changed. I would also invite selected friends to comment on my findings. Connecting these responses with my original coding would help me identify assumptions and make the development of my understanding visible.
